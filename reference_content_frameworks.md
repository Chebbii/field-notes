Living log of outside frameworks worth adapting into planner content or product features. Each entry: plain-language explanation, verified source, concrete application idea. No duplicates — check existing entries before adding.

## New, verified 2026-08-30

### Implementation Intentions ("If-Then Planning")
Psychologist Peter Gollwitzer's research shows that turning a vague goal into a specific "if [situation], then I will [action]" statement makes people far more likely to follow through — a meta-analysis of 94 studies found a medium-to-large effect (d = .65) on goal attainment, and a later 642-study analysis confirmed the effect holds across cognitive, emotional, and behavioral goals. The mechanism: pre-deciding the trigger removes the need for in-the-moment willpower or memory, which is exactly where ADHD executive function struggles.
Source: [If-then planning, Taylor & Francis review](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10463283.2020.1808936); [Implementation Intentions and Goal Achievement meta-analysis](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/37367696_Implementation_Intentions_and_Goal_Achievement_A_Meta-Analysis_of_Effects_and_Processes)
Product application: Replace open-ended "goals" fields in the planner with a forced if-then micro-prompt template ("If it's 9am and I haven't opened my laptop, then I do a 2-minute desk reset") — one per daily page, tied to the user's stated transition goal.

### Temptation Bundling
Wharton professor Katy Milkman's 2014 randomized field experiment paired an instantly-gratifying "want" (audiobooks/page-turner novels) with a valuable but avoided "should" (gym visits), restricting access to the want to only-while-doing-the-should. Result: 51% more gym visits during the study, with a 2020 follow-up showing elevated workout frequency persisting 17 weeks after the intervention ended.
Source: [Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym (SSRN)](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2183859); [Knowledge at Wharton coverage](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/researchers-used-hunger-games-encourage-healthier-choices/)
Product application: A planner module that has users explicitly pair one dreaded task with one guilty-pleasure item ("only listen to [podcast] while doing [avoided admin task]"), logged as a locked pair rather than two separate to-dos — makes the bundling a structural constraint, not just a suggestion.

## New, verified 2026-09-02

### WOOP / Mental Contrasting with Implementation Intentions (MCII)
NYU psychologist Gabriele Oettingen's four-step protocol — Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan — combines vividly imagining a desired outcome ("mental contrasting") with an if-then plan for the specific obstacle that will get in the way. Her research base (25+ years, including RCTs on weight management, physical activity in stroke survivors, and VA weight-loss programs) found that pure positive visualization alone can backfire — the brain treats the imagined success as already achieved and reduces the effort put toward it — while contrasting that image against a named obstacle, then planning for it, reliably outperforms wishful thinking alone.
Source: [Gabriele Oettingen's Publications, NYU Motivation Lab](https://wp.nyu.edu/motivationlab/publications/gabriele-oettingen/); [What Is Mental Contrasting and How Can We Benefit From It? (Positive Psychology)](https://positivepsychology.com/mental-contrasting/)
Product application: A "reality-check" variant of the planner's goal page that forces four short fields in order — Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan — instead of the more common "just visualize success" prompt seen in most planners; positions the brand against toxic-positivity goal-setting content, which is a natural talking point for the life-transition audience.

### Plan-Making Eliminates Intrusive Thoughts About Unfinished Goals
Building on the classic Zeigarnik effect (unfinished tasks are remembered better and intrude on thought more than finished ones), a 2011 study by E.J. Masicampo and Roy Baumeister (Journal of Personality and Social Psychology) found that writing a specific when-and-how plan for an unfinished goal — without actually completing it — reduced intrusive thoughts and cognitive interference on an unrelated task just as effectively as finishing the goal did. The plan itself, not the completion, is what quiets the mental "open loop."
Source: [Consider It Done! Plan Making Can Eliminate the Cognitive Effects of Unfulfilled Goals, Masicampo & Baumeister 2011 (PDF)](https://users.wfu.edu/masicaej/MasicampoBaumeister2011JPSP.pdf)
Product application: A nightly "brain dump → schedule it" ritual page: any task logged as unfinished at day's end must get a specific day/time slot before the page can be marked closed — directly targeting the racing-thoughts-at-bedtime complaint common in ADHD and life-transition audiences, and distinct from Implementation Intentions above (that entry is about follow-through rate; this one is about relieving rumination even before the task is done).

## New, verified 2026-09-03

### Bridges' Transition Model (Ending, Neutral Zone, New Beginning)
Organizational consultant William Bridges' framework, introduced in his 1991 book *Managing Transitions*, distinguishes "change" (an external, situational event) from "transition" (the internal, psychological process of coming to terms with it). It names three phases people move through on any transition: Ending/Losing/Letting Go (grieving what's over), the Neutral Zone (an disorienting in-between where the old identity is gone but the new one isn't yet functional — the phase people find hardest and most tempting to rush), and the New Beginning (rebuilding competence and committing to the new normal). Though built for workplace change management, it's widely applied to personal life transitions (divorce, career change, relocation, empty-nesting).
Source: [Bridges Transition Model, William Bridges Associates (official)](https://wmbridges.com/about/what-is-transition/); [Bridges' Transition Model explained, Mindtools](https://www.mindtools.com/afhbe6s/bridges-transition-model/)
Product application: A "which phase am I in?" self-assessment as a planner front-matter page, with phase-specific spreads that follow — an Ending spread built around naming/logging what's being let go rather than jumping straight to goal-setting, and an explicit "Neutral Zone" spread that normalizes the disorientation instead of implying the user should already have a plan. Directly names the messy middle stage most planners skip past, which is a strong differentiator for the life-transition audience.

### Fogg Behavior Model (B = MAP)
Stanford researcher BJ Fogg's model, formalized in a 2009 paper and popularized in his 2019 book *Tiny Habits*, holds that a behavior only happens when three things converge at the same moment: sufficient Motivation, sufficient Ability (how easy/simple the action is right now), and a Prompt (a cue to act). Fogg's central design claim, now cited in 1,900+ academic publications: motivation is unreliable and hard to engineer, so the far more effective lever is Ability — shrink the target behavior until it's easy enough to do even on a low-motivation, low-executive-function moment, rather than trying to psych the user up.
Source: [Fogg Behavior Model, official site (behaviormodel.org)](https://www.behaviormodel.org/); [Fogg Behavior Model: Motivation, Ability, and Prompts, Northbeam](https://www.northbeam.io/blog/fogg-behavior-model-motivation-ability-and-prompts)
Product application: Reframe the planner's task-sizing prompts around Ability rather than Motivation — instead of "how bad do you want this," ask "what's the smallest version of this you could do in under 2 minutes," paired with an anchor+prompt slot ("right after [existing routine], I will..."). This is a distinct lever from Implementation Intentions (which pre-decides the trigger) and Temptation Bundling (which pre-decides the reward): this one is specifically about shrinking task difficulty, which maps directly onto ADHD task-initiation struggles.

## New, verified 2026-09-06

### Self-Compassion (Neff's Three Components)
Psychologist Kristin Neff's research program defines self-compassion as three paired components: self-kindness (vs. harsh self-judgment) when something goes wrong, common humanity (vs. isolation) — recognizing struggle and failure as part of shared human experience rather than a personal defect, and mindfulness (vs. over-identification) — holding painful feelings in balanced awareness instead of being swept up in them. Her peer-reviewed research base, synthesized in a 2023 Annual Review of Psychology paper, links higher self-compassion to lower anxiety/depression and — notably for a planner audience — to *more* motivation and resilience after failure, not less, contradicting the common fear that self-kindness makes people complacent.
Source: [Self-Compassion: Theory, Method, Research, and Intervention, Annual Review of Psychology 2023](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-psych-032420-031047); [The Science of Self-Compassion, Kristin Neff (PDF)](https://sati.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Article-Neff-Science-of-Self-Compassion-Review.pdf)
Product application: Replace the planner's "missed task" review with a three-field "self-compassion break" (mirroring the three components: "this is a hard moment," "I'm not the only one who struggles with this," "what's one kind thing I can do for myself right now") that must be completed before a missed task gets rescheduled — reframes a missed day as data rather than a moral failure. Distinct from Bridges' Transition Model above, which addresses identity-level upheaval; this targets the smaller, daily shame spiral that derails ADHD task follow-through specifically.

### Transtheoretical Model / Stages of Change
Psychologists James Prochaska and Carlo DiClemente's model (developed from the 1980s onward) holds that readiness for a specific behavior change moves through stages — precontemplation (not yet seeing a need to change), contemplation (weighing pros and cons, still ambivalent), preparation (intending to act soon), action, and maintenance — and that movement is often cyclical rather than a straight line, with relapse to an earlier stage being a normal part of the process rather than a failure of it.
Source: [Transtheoretical Model: Stages of Health Behavior Change, Simply Psychology](https://www.simplypsychology.org/transtheoretical-model.html); [The transtheoretical model of health behavior change, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10170434/)
Product application: A per-habit (not per-life-transition) "what stage am I actually in for this?" check-in that changes which planner tools get offered — Precontemplation/Contemplation stages get reflection prompts only (no streak trackers or action plans), Preparation gets the if-then templates, Action/Maintenance get streak tracking and relapse-prevention plans. This is distinct from Bridges' Transition Model (which maps the emotional arc of one big life change) by operating at the level of a single specific habit, and it directly prevents the common planner failure of pushing action-stage tools onto someone who hasn't yet decided they want to change that particular thing — a frequent source of guilt and abandoned trackers.

## New, verified 2026-09-09

### Self-Determination Theory (Autonomy, Competence, Relatedness)
Psychologists Edward Deci and Richard Ryan's decades-long research program (formalized across the 1980s-2000s and still actively extended) holds that motivation quality, not just quantity, depends on three innate psychological needs being met: autonomy (acting with a genuine sense of choice, not coercion), competence (feeling capable and seeing mastery build), and relatedness (feeling connected to others). Across thousands of studies in education, sports, and workplace settings, motivation driven by these needs ("intrinsic" or well-internalized motivation) produces more persistence and well-being than motivation driven by external pressure, guilt, or reward-chasing — directly relevant to a planner audience prone to shame-based self-management after a life disruption.
Source: [Self-Determination Theory and the Facilitation of Intrinsic Motivation, Social Development, and Well-Being, Ryan & Deci 2000 (PDF)](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2000_RyanDeci_SDT.pdf); [Self-determination theory: A quarter century of human motivation research, APA](https://www.apa.org/research-practice/conduct-research/self-determination-theory)
Product application: An "autonomy check" on planner task templates — before a task is logged, a toggle asking whether it was chosen or imposed, with imposed tasks routed toward reframing prompts that surface some element of choice (why, when, or how) rather than just tracking completion; pairs with small "competence" markers (visible mastery notes, not streak-shaming) and an optional relatedness prompt ("who could know about this today"). Distinct from Fogg's Ability lever and Implementation Intentions' trigger lever above — this targets the quality/source of motivation itself, which matters most for the audience segment burning out on willpower-based planners.

### SCARF Model (Status, Certainty, Autonomy, Relatedness, Fairness)
Neuroscience-informed leadership researcher David Rock introduced SCARF in 2008 (NeuroLeadership Journal, refreshed by his institute in 2025) to describe five social domains — Status, Certainty, Autonomy, Relatedness, Fairness — that the brain treats with the same threat-or-reward machinery as physical survival needs. A perceived hit to any one domain (e.g., losing certainty during a job change) triggers a genuine threat response that narrows thinking and raises defensiveness, while restoring even one domain (e.g., naming what is still certain) can measurably calm that response.
Source: [The Evolution of the Social Brain: Introducing SCARF in 2025, NeuroLeadership Institute (official)](https://www.neuroleadership.com/articles/the-evolution-of-the-social-brain-introducing-scarf-in-2025); [David Rock's SCARF Model, Mindtools](https://www.mindtools.com/akswgc0/david-rocks-scarf-model/)
Product application: A "which domain got hit today" check-in specifically for the Neutral Zone phase of Bridges' Transition Model (logged above) — instead of generic "how are you feeling," name which of the five domains (often Certainty and Status during a life transition) took the hit, then offer a domain-specific micro-repair prompt (for Certainty: "name one thing that hasn't changed"; for Status: "name one thing you're still recognized for"). Gives the brand's transition content a concrete neuroscience vocabulary distinct from the purely emotional framing of grief/change-curve content.

## New, verified 2026-09-10

### Process Praise vs. Person Praise (Mueller & Dweck, 1998)
A controlled set of six studies with fifth-graders by Claudia Mueller and Carol Dweck found that praising a child's intelligence/ability after a success ("you're so smart") backfired compared to praising their effort/process ("you must have worked hard"): the ability-praised group cared more about looking competent than about learning, and after a subsequent failure showed less persistence, less enjoyment of the task, more likely to blame their own low ability, and worse performance on a follow-up task — while the effort-praised group stayed persistent and described intelligence as something that improves with work rather than a fixed trait. This is the specific, well-replicated experimental finding underneath the later, more broadly popularized (and now contested) "growth mindset" framing — a 2018 meta-analysis (Sisk et al., Psychological Science, 273 studies) found only weak overall effects for general mindset interventions, and a 2023 re-analysis restricted to preregistered/blinded studies found no significant effect at all — so this entry leans on the narrower, still-solid praise-language finding rather than the oversold general claim.
Source: [Praise for Intelligence Can Undermine Children's Motivation and Performance, Mueller & Dweck 1998, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9686450/); [full text PDF, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 75(1)](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/psychology/courses/3615/Readings/Mueller_Dweck.pdf)
Product application: Rewrite the planner's built-in affirmation/completion copy away from trait praise ("you're so disciplined," "you're a planner person") and toward process-specific reflection prompts ("what approach did you try today," "what did you keep doing even when it was hard") — especially in the missed-task/failure flow, where trait praise is exactly backwards. Distinct from the Self-Compassion entry above (which addresses how a user talks to themselves after failure); this is about how the product's own copy praises success, which sets up whether a later failure reads as "I'm just not that kind of person" or "that approach didn't work, try another."

### Self-Efficacy Theory and Its Four Sources (Bandura, 1977)
Stanford psychologist Albert Bandura's theory, formalized in a 1977 Psychological Review paper and still a live, heavily cited research area (APA maintains an active research page on it), defines self-efficacy as a person's belief in their own capacity to execute the specific actions needed to produce a specific outcome — distinct from generic self-esteem, and distinct from merely believing an action would work (outcome expectancy) since someone can believe a strategy works while still doubting they can personally pull it off. Bandura ranked four sources that build or erode this belief: mastery experiences (actually succeeding at the task — the strongest lever), vicarious experiences (watching someone similar succeed), verbal/social persuasion (credible encouragement from others), and physiological/affective states (reading your own racing heart or fatigue as information about your capability, for better or worse).
Source: [Self-efficacy: The theory at the heart of human agency, American Psychological Association](https://www.apa.org/research-practice/conduct-research/self-efficacy-human-agency); [Self-Efficacy: Bandura's Theory Of Motivation, Simply Psychology](https://www.simplypsychology.org/self-efficacy.html)
Product application: Build the planner's low-motivation/relapse-recovery flow around all four levers deliberately rather than generic "believe in yourself" copy — open with a deliberately easy first task sized for a guaranteed win (mastery), include a short real-user example matched to the same struggle (vicarious), pair it with one specific, credible line of encouragement tied to something the user has actually done before (verbal persuasion), and add a reframing prompt for pre-task dread ("that jittery feeling is your body gearing up, not a stop sign") to recast physiological arousal as readiness rather than a threat signal. Distinct from Self-Determination Theory's "competence" need already logged above (one of three needs in a broader motivation theory); this is a dedicated single-construct theory with four concrete, separately actionable design levers.

## New, verified 2026-09-13

### Motivational Interviewing (OARS + Evocation, Miller & Rollnick)
Clinical psychologists William Miller and Stephen Rollnick's counseling style, developed from the 1980s onward, works through four core skills (Open questions, Affirmations, Reflections, Summaries — "OARS") aimed at drawing out a person's own reasons for change ("change talk") rather than persuading or advising them into it. A key, counterintuitive finding of their later research: directly weighing pros-and-cons ("decisional balance") can backfire with someone who's genuinely ambivalent, since debating the cons out loud strengthens the person's own case against changing — whereas evocation (asking open questions that elicit the person's own change talk) increases commitment instead. Two meta-analyses of the RCT evidence (Lundahl et al. 2010, 25 years of studies; a 2013 medical-settings meta-analysis of 48 RCTs, n=9,618) found a modest but statistically significant advantage for MI across behaviors including substance use, physical activity, and treatment adherence.
Source: [Understanding Motivational Interviewing, Motivational Interviewing Network of Trainers (MINT, official)](https://motivationalinterviewing.org/understanding-motivational-interviewing); [A Meta-Analysis of Motivational Interviewing: Twenty-Five Years of Empirical Studies, Lundahl et al. 2010, Research on Social Work Practice](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1049731509347850)
Product application: Rewrite the planner's ambivalence-flagged check-ins (e.g., someone marked "Contemplation" under the Transtheoretical Model entry above) as OARS-style open prompts that elicit the user's own reasons ("what would be different for you if this changed?", "what's making this matter to you right now?") instead of a pros/cons worksheet or motivational copy telling them why they should act — the decisional-balance finding above argues directly against the pros/cons format many planners default to for someone still on the fence. Distinct from Self-Determination Theory's autonomy need (a broader motivational construct) — this is a specific conversational/prompt-design technique for the fence-sitting moment itself.

### Urge Surfing (Marlatt's Mindfulness-Based Relapse Prevention)
Psychologist G. Alan Marlatt introduced this technique in the 1980s as part of his cognitive-behavioral relapse-prevention model: instead of fighting or suppressing a craving/urge, the person is coached to observe it like an ocean wave — noticing it build, peak, and pass on its own within a few minutes if not acted on — rather than treating the urge as something that will keep intensifying until it's acted on. Marlatt and colleague Sarah Bowen tested a brief version of this with college-student smokers, and a later trial (Witkiewitz et al. 2017) found the technique didn't necessarily reduce the urges themselves but changed how people responded to them, resulting in fewer cigarettes smoked — evidence that the value is in changing the response to an urge, not eliminating the urge.
Source: [Relapse Prevention: An Overview of Marlatt's Cognitive-Behavioral Model, PMC (NIH)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6760427/); [Surfing the Urge: Brief Mindfulness-Based Intervention for College Student Smokers, Bowen & Marlatt (Semantic Scholar record)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Surfing-the-urge:-brief-mindfulness-based-for-Bowen-Marlatt/7bae69efb7ba32438ead7be638d8b584e18bcfd7)
Product application: An in-the-moment "ride it out" micro-prompt for the specific moment a user feels the urge to abandon a task or task-switch mid-work (a common ADHD derailment point) — a 60-90 second timer with copy naming that the pull to quit/switch will crest and fall on its own, rather than a willpower message. Distinct from Temptation Bundling above (which pre-arranges a reward before the urge even arises) and Self-Compassion above (which addresses the aftermath of a slip) — this one targets the live moment of impulse itself, before the derailing action happens.
